## Reading for a feeling she couldn’t get back

Around forty, Anna Lembke gets absorbed in *Twilight*. She likes the fantasy and the romance, and the story offers relief during a difficult transition. Her children are becoming teenagers. She is grieving the fact that she won’t have another baby, and she and her husband disagree about it. It is easy to spend an evening inside a story that gives her a break from all of that.

She reads the series four times. Each reread gives her less pleasure and leaves her wanting more of what she felt the first time. She looks for other paranormal romances and then more explicit books. A Kindle makes the next book instant and lets her read almost anywhere. Soon she is skipping ahead to the scene she wants, reading through the night, and even bringing the Kindle to work. Eventually she struggles to find a romance novel she enjoys, and the habit is still hard to put down.

## Saying it aloud

While teaching psychiatry residents how to ask about habits, Lembke takes the patient role in a practice conversation. She tells a student she stays up too late reading. He asks what she would give up if she stopped. She says immediately that she loves the escape. As she talks, she hears what she’s been having trouble seeing. The books are cutting into her sleep and affecting how she shows up at work and with her children.

She gets rid of the Kindle and takes a month away from romance novels. In the first weeks, the evenings are especially difficult. She lies on the couch with a sense of dread and has trouble sleeping at the hour when she used to read. With time, she becomes more able to stay in the moment without immediately looking for another story.

## What she wanted to come back to

At the end of the month, she tries reading romance again in moderation. Two late nights leave her exhausted. She recognizes the pattern much sooner this time, and seeing it clearly takes some of the fun out of it. Giving up the Kindle adds a practical pause. A trip to the library or bookstore takes more effort than downloading the next novel in bed.

In the conclusion, Lembke describes giving more attention to the parts of patient care she finds rewarding. She follows people over time and listens closely as their stories unfold. The work gives her something to be absorbed in that also matters to her after the day is over. She describes professional success as a byproduct of returning to that part of the work.
