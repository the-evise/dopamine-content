## Leaving engineering

At college, David is afraid that if he asks a question, everyone will see how much he doesn’t know. Speaking in front of people makes him sweat, and his thoughts become harder to organize. A clinic diagnoses him with attention deficit disorder and anxiety. He starts taking Paxil and Adderall. The first helps a little with the physical symptoms of anxiety, and the second helps him focus. He still finds it hard to speak up when he falls behind. After trying a less interactive program, he leaves engineering for history, where he can do more work on his own.

Adderall also makes it possible to postpone studying and then work through the night before a test. Over time, David comes to feel he can’t study without it. He starts taking more.

## Keeping up at a startup

After graduation David follows his girlfriend to Palo Alto, and they marry. At a tech startup, colleagues teach him the programming he had wanted to learn in college. He becomes a software developer. From outside, he looks like someone who has found his footing. He works long days and runs in his spare time.

His days increasingly depend on stimulants and caffeine to keep going, medication to sleep, and another prescription for presentation anxiety. He keeps the extent of his use from his wife. Exhaustion and difficulty concentrating feel like further reasons to take medication. When he begins thinking about suicide, he tells his pregnant wife he needs help and asks her to take him to the hospital. She learns there how much he has been taking. He spends two weeks in inpatient care and goes through withdrawal before coming home.

## The conversations he used to avoid

After discharge, David starts exposure therapy. One assignment is to introduce himself to coworkers in common areas. Before an early conversation, he rates his anxiety at the top of his scale. He expects to look foolish or to have someone call security. A few minutes later, he’s had an ordinary conversation and his anxiety has fallen.

Next he starts ordering coffee at the counter, where he used to use an app so he could avoid talking. When he spills some coffee and feels embarrassed, his therapist helps him practice tolerating that feeling. With time, he plans less of his day around avoiding people. He can get on a crowded train without waiting for a quieter one, and he starts enjoying some of the conversations he used to dread.
