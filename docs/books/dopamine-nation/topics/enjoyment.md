# Enjoyment and its opposite

*Dopamine Nation* · Chapters 1–6

The first six chapters distinguish looking forward to an activity, enjoying it as it happens, feeling relieved by it, and feeling satisfied afterward. The same activity can feel different at each of those points.

## Chapter 1 · Our Masturbation Machines

### Anticipation can outlast enjoyment

Chi is excited while choosing a purchase and waiting for it to arrive. His excitement peaks when he opens the package and fades shortly after he sees the object. Ordering inexpensive items and immediately returning them lets him repeat the part he enjoys. Jacob also looks forward to the experience he’s setting up, even though he repeatedly finds that the reality falls short of what he imagined.

### Enjoyment can narrow to one moment

Lembke first enjoys *Twilight* as a whole story. Later, she reads erotic novels for the moment the sexual tension resolves. She skips the characters, plot, and writing she once cared about. The immediate payoff still draws her in while the routine takes time away from sleep, family, and socializing.

### Pleasure and distress can coexist

Jacob enjoys building and experimenting with his device, as well as the connection he finds online. The wider pattern also leaves him ashamed, isolated, and desperate to stop. Enjoyment at one point in the experience can coexist with distress about the routine and its consequences.

## Chapter 2 · Running from Pain

### Relief can feel rewarding

David’s routine initially gives him focus and a way to get through uncomfortable situations. Over time, he relies on stimulants to keep going and sedatives to sleep. Kevin gives himself what he wants in the moment, then admits he’s unhappy with how his life is going. Both stories show how immediate relief or pleasure can come apart from later satisfaction.

### Boredom can give way to quiet enjoyment

Sophie initially finds the walk to class without audio boring. After getting used to the quiet, she begins noticing the trees and comes to like the walk. Lembke also describes boredom as space in which new thoughts can form.

### Avoiding discomfort can cost you something you enjoy

David leaves engineering, a subject he loves, because speaking up and asking for help feel too painful. Years later, he rediscovers coding in a workplace where colleagues are willing to help him learn.

## Chapter 3 · The Pleasure-Pain Balance

### Wanting can persist as enjoyment fades

Dopamine helps motivate the pursuit of a reward. Lembke’s rereading of *Twilight* illustrates the difference. She enjoyed each reread less and felt more eager to recover the original feeling afterward. Eventually, she had trouble enjoying romance novels at all and still found the habit difficult to drop.

### Anticipation and the aftermath shape the experience

A cue can make a future reward feel close enough to pursue. When the expected payoff fails to arrive, the anticipation can give way to disappointment. Even when the reward arrives, Lembke’s opponent-process model describes how pleasure can be followed by restlessness, dissatisfaction, or craving.

### Pain and pleasure can overlap

Spicy food can hurt and taste good at the same time. Beecher’s wounded soldiers and the construction worker with a nail through his boot illustrate how the meaning of an event can affect how much pain someone feels.

### Ordinary pleasures can return

Lembke uses anhedonia to describe diminished enjoyment and explains how everyday rewards can feel flat during heavy, prolonged substance use. She describes people regaining pleasure in ordinary experiences, such as a walk or a meal with friends, as they readapt during abstinence.

## Chapter 4 · Dopamine Fasting

### A break can reveal enjoyment you were missing

Delilah discovers that she enjoys some family events more sober. She also feels relieved to have her attention back after spending so much time arranging her next chance to get high. She still misses the creativity and escape she associates with cannabis.

### Quiet can become easier to tolerate

Without her evening romance novels, Lembke initially feels dread. With time, she becomes more able to be present without immediately looking for something to occupy her mind. Understanding what the books offered her, including escape from a difficult family transition, helps explain why she kept reaching for them.

## Chapter 5 · Space, Time, and Meaning

### Relief can keep you in the activity

Jacob describes the long sessions with his machine as a trance. While he’s absorbed, he stops thinking about his family problems. Afterward, he wants to stop and finds himself repeating the routine.

### How you get an activity can affect how long you stay with it

After giving up the Kindle, Lembke can still read fiction. Going to a bookstore or library puts a pause before starting another book and helps her spend less time reading beyond what she intended.

### Limits can leave more room for other pleasures

Nearly six months after his relapse, Jacob describes being able to choose what he does again. He enjoys seeing his wife and looks forward to Christmas with her.

## Chapter 6 · A Broken Balance?

### Relief can make room for things you care about

When Chris was using opioids, his sense of connection faded when the effects wore off. With his cravings more manageable during treatment, he could engage in research, build relationships, and look forward to seeing his wife.

### A wider emotional range includes difficult feelings

Lembke describes a patient who valued being able to grieve even when anxiety and depression returned. Another patient’s husband noticed that she enjoyed listening to music again after tapering off long-term opioid pain medication. Lembke recalls getting along with her mother more easily while taking Prozac and valuing the curiosity and questioning that returned when she stopped. The people in these accounts weigh relief and emotional range differently.
