# The Molecule of More — Chapter 1: Love

*Reading notes on Daniel Z. Lieberman and Michael E. Long’s The Molecule of More.*

## The central idea

**Losing the thrill of discovery can change how an experience feels, even when the experience itself is still good.** The authors use romantic love to explore how the excitement of a possibility gives way to the experience of actually living with it.

Their bakery example makes this easy to see. You spot a bakery on a street you walk every day, go inside, and find coffee and a croissant far better than you expected. You come back for weeks. One morning, you realize the visit no longer feels special. The coffee and croissant can be just as good. What changed is how much they surprise you.

## The story of Shawn and Samantha

Shawn and Samantha meet at a bar and find each other unexpectedly easy to talk to. They’re excited by what they’re discovering and by what they imagine could happen between them. Once they’ve been together a while, that sense of possibility fades. When Samantha runs into an old boyfriend, she feels a sudden rush of excitement and starts wondering what she’s missing with Shawn.

The chapter later shows them with a baby. They help each other make time for work and study, steal quiet minutes together, and enjoy each other’s company without needing to talk. When Shawn reaches for her in the dark, he just wants to know she’s there. The authors use those scenes to illustrate **companionate love**. Familiarity, which took away some of the early surprise, is now part of what feels good.

## The dopamine concepts

| Concept | What the chapter shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Reward prediction error** | In the animal experiments the book describes, certain dopamine neurons respond when a reward is better than expected. Once a signal reliably predicts food, the response shifts toward the signal. |
| **Anticipation** | A signal can feel compelling before you know what you’ll get. The chapter links that pull to the appeal of unpredictable rewards, including slot machines. |
| **Glamour** | A distant mountain can look like the perfect adventure. The imagined climb leaves out the cold, fatigue, and difficulty. The authors use *glamour* for the appeal of a possibility whose details you can still fill in. |
| **Present enjoyment** | The book’s *Here & Nows* framework describes savoring what’s already there, such as the taste of the croissant, the comfort of a favorite place, or the ease of being with someone familiar. |

## What this adds to Dopamine Personality

For the **Novelty ↔ Familiarity** axis, separate what makes someone **choose** an experience from what makes them **enjoy** it. The same person might love scouting a new café and get the most pleasure from their usual order at a place they know well.
